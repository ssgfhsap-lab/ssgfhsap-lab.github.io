A life, counted in orbits

You are here.

Every year of your life is one trip around the sun, on a small planet, third out from an ordinary star, carrying everyone who has ever lived. You did not choose to board it, and it will not stop to let you off.

Zoom in until you find a single path across a single yard. Zoom out until the sun is one grain among billions. Both are true at once, and you are somewhere in between.

The theory

Time eats itself.

now, the mouth · 0 seconds eaten since you opened this pagegone

The present is the mouth. It swallows the next moment, and what is left behind is the past. Nothing is stored. The only time that exists is the piece being eaten right now.

So everything I do is food for it. The work, the plans, the waiting. I cannot save any of it. I can only choose what it eats.

How the fading is worked out: a standard mortality curve (Gompertz–Makeham), where the yearly risk of death roughly doubles every seven years, set to match typical life tables for a wealthy country. It is an average over many people. It knows nothing about any one person, so read it as the shape of the odds and not a prediction. One orbit is taken as 940 million km. In the picture the planets keep their real order and relative speeds, with Earth taking one minute per lap. Distances and sizes are squeezed so everything fits; at true scale the planets would be invisible specks. The street map is drawn live from OpenStreetMap data (fetched through the Overpass API when the page opens), so it stays sharp however far you zoom in and shows exactly what mappers have recorded around this part of Pakistan, which in places is sparse. The Earth, Moon, planets and star field use real imagery and are rendered at your screen's resolution. The Milky Way is NASA's illustration, because nobody has photographed our galaxy from outside. Andromeda is a real photograph. The Earth and Moon view is to scale; the jumps in size between views are compressed, since the real ones are far larger.
